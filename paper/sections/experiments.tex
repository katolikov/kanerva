\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}

Sections~\ref{sec:dev} to~\ref{sec:second} are the record of the method before version 0.5.0: its development results and its first two held-out tests. They are kept as they were run, and their models, thresholds and lexical rows are not those of the current release. The current release is measured in Section~\ref{sec:third} and the sections after it, up to and including Section~\ref{sec:online}.

\subsection{Setup}
\paragraph{Mode.} No LLM takes part in the method. Metrics are retrieval-level, so we report what reaches the context and not what a model does with it, except in Section~\ref{sec:answers}, where a small local model answers from the selected notes.

\paragraph{Development data.} \emph{LongMemEval-S} \cite{wu2025longmemeval} has 500 questions, each with its own store of about 490 turns. \emph{LoCoMo} \cite{maharana2024locomo} has 10 long conversations. \emph{BEAM-1M} \cite{tavakoli2026beam} has 35 long conversations of about 2,000 turns. \emph{RepoMem} (new) turns eight Python repositories into memory streams, at 14 snapshots over six years. Each interval is a session in which a simulated agent records commit notes and facts observed in changed files. Facts (constants, signatures, symbol locations, dependency constraints) come from \texttt{ast}, so a fact is stale exactly when the same key held another value at an earlier snapshot. Fact queries ask for the current value; task queries use a real later commit subject, with earlier commits on the same files as gold. RepoMem runs with slot keys, as a host agent would supply, and without.

\paragraph{First held-out test.} \emph{BEAM-100K and BEAM-500K}: \V{n-beam} questions in 55 conversations that share no seed with BEAM-1M, keeping every question with labelled evidence. Their turns average \V{goldtok-beam} tokens for evidence and \V{othertok-beam} for the rest, and the full history of a conversation averages \V{full-beam} tokens. \emph{RepoMem} on five repositories outside development (marshmallow, pluggy, packaging, loguru, tqdm): \V{n-repomem-fresh} queries over three seeded streams each. No model, threshold or design choice was made on this data. The method was frozen first, and the test ran once, with a single script.

\paragraph{Baselines.} Recency, BM25 (FTS5), dense (all-MiniLM-L6-v2 \cite{hf_minilm}), hybrid RRF \cite{cormack2009rrf}, hybrid followed by a cross-encoder (ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2) on the top 50, MMR \cite{carbonell1998mmr}, Generative Agents scoring \cite{park2023generative} with constant importance, facility location under a knapsack as in PACMS \cite{pacms2026}, hybrid with superseded versions removed (temporal invalidation alone, in the spirit of \cite{rasmussen2025zep}), hybrid listed newest first, and hybrid keeping only the newest of near-twins. We also keep the \emph{first version} of the method: a linear prior over retrieval signals with no item, role or context features, and a pool of 100 from each retriever. All methods share candidate generators, token counts (\texttt{cl100k\_base}) and a 14-token per-item overhead, and every ranking is filled greedily to the budget, which already makes the baselines budget-aware. Systems that need an LLM on write were not run.

\paragraph{Statistics.} The independent unit is a conversation on BEAM (55) and a repository on RepoMem (5 in the first test), since queries within a conversation are dependent and seeds repeat RepoMem queries. We give bootstrap intervals on the unit, and $p$-values from two-sided sign-flip permutation tests on the unit means, with Holm correction across budgets within a comparison. With five units the smallest possible two-sided $p$ is $1/16$, so no RepoMem comparison can reach significance. There, read the interval and the count of units favouring each side, and note that an interval containing zero on five units is weak evidence that two methods are equal.

\subsection{Development results of the earlier version}\label{sec:dev}
Table~\ref{tab:dev} gives cross-fitted recall: each query is scored by a model that never saw its question, conversation or repository. The relevance model raises mean recall over the first version on all four datasets, from \V{dev-lme-first-version-mean} to \V{dev-lme-kanerva-mean} on LongMemEval-S, \V{dev-locomo-first-version-mean} to \V{dev-locomo-kanerva-mean} on LoCoMo, \V{dev-repomem-first-version-mean} to \V{dev-repomem-kanerva-mean} on RepoMem and \V{dev-beam-dev-first-version-mean} to \V{dev-beam-dev-kanerva-mean} on BEAM-1M. The large-budget loss to hybrid ranking disappears on LoCoMo: \V{dev-locomo-kanerva-4096} against \V{dev-locomo-hybrid-4096} at 4096 tokens. Without a cross-encoder the method still trails the reranker at the smallest budget, \V{dev-locomo-kanerva-128} against \V{dev-locomo-rerank-128} on LoCoMo at 128 tokens, because at that size one item decides everything and only joint reading of query and text separates the top candidates. With cross-encoder features it closes that gap, at \V{dev-locomo-kanerva-ce-128}.

The candidate pool matters most on BEAM, where a question has many evidence turns. There the top 100 from each retriever reach \V{pool-beam-dev-pool100} of the evidence, and 200 reach \V{pool-beam-dev-pool200}, at a cost of \V{pool-beam-dev-size200} candidates instead of \V{pool-beam-dev-size100}. Adding the neighbours of the 50 best hits reaches \V{pool-beam-dev-nb50x1} with \V{pool-beam-dev-nb50x1_size} candidates, so neighbours add about twice as much evidence per candidate as a deeper pool, while pseudo-relevance feedback adds little (\V{pool-beam-dev-prf}).

Table~\ref{tab:devablation} removes one feature group at a time and refits. Item features and neighbourhood features carry most of the gain, and query--item overlap adds little on top of them.

The cross-fitted probabilities are close to calibrated: over ten equal-count bins the expected calibration error is \V{ece-lme} on LongMemEval-S, \V{ece-locomo} on LoCoMo, \V{ece-repomem} on RepoMem and \V{ece-beam-dev} on BEAM-1M, against base rates near one percent. They are somewhat overconfident at the top on BEAM-1M, where the highest-scored one percent of candidates are predicted at \V{top-beam-dev-predicted} and observed at \V{top-beam-dev-observed}.

With such weights, the choice of knapsack solver hardly matters: over the four datasets, mean recall is \V{rule-lme-density} for density greedy against \V{rule-lme-dp} for an exact knapsack on LongMemEval-S, and \V{rule-beam-dev-density} against \V{rule-beam-dev-dp} on BEAM-1M. Cost still matters where item lengths vary: ranking by probability alone falls to \V{rule-beam-dev-rank} on BEAM-1M. We keep density greedy because Theorem~\ref{thm:selection} covers it.

\paragraph{Code history.} On RepoMem the picture is mixed. The method leads at the smallest budget, with keys \R{repomem-fresh}{kanerva}{128} against \R{repomem-fresh}{rerank}{128} for the reranker and \R{repomem-fresh}{hybrid-live}{128} for hybrid with invalidation. The first version is beaten on every repository, by \V{d-repomem-fresh-plain-kanerva_vs_kanerva-r1-mean} without keys. Most of the loss at large budgets sits on the task track, and it comes from the near-twin rules, which take look-alike commit notes for versions of one fact. Without keys at 4096 tokens, task recall is \V{track-repomem-fresh-plain-task-kanerva-4096}, against \V{track-repomem-fresh-plain-task--supersession-4096} with those rules removed and \V{track-repomem-fresh-plain-task-hybrid-4096} for hybrid, while fact recall is \V{track-repomem-fresh-plain-fact-kanerva-4096} against \V{track-repomem-fresh-plain-fact-hybrid-4096}, which accounts for the rest. Removing the rules altogether would bring back the stale injections shown next, so we restricted them to facts and ran a second held-out test (Section~\ref{sec:second}).

\paragraph{Lighter configurations.} The lexical model needs no embeddings, and it was the plugin default at the time. Its rows in this section and the next two were computed on stores that held vectors, so they compared near-twins by vectors, which a real lexical store cannot do (Section~\ref{sec:correction}). On BEAM it has \R{beam}{kanerva-lex}{mean} against \R{beam}{bm25}{mean} for BM25, on the same candidates. On RepoMem without keys it has \R{repomem-fresh-plain}{kanerva-lex}{mean} against \R{repomem-fresh-plain}{bm25}{mean}, and it loses to BM25 at 4096 tokens, \R{repomem-fresh-plain}{kanerva-lex}{4096} against \R{repomem-fresh-plain}{bm25}{4096}. With the static model2vec embedder \cite{model2vec}, which needs no neural runtime, the method reaches \R{beam-static}{kanerva}{mean} on BEAM, better than the lexical model, but \R{repomem-fresh-static}{kanerva}{mean} on RepoMem, worse than the lexical model and worse than BM25. On changed RepoMem facts it also injects a stale version more often, \V{st-repomem-fresh-static-kanerva-stale_any} against \V{st-repomem-fresh-plain-kanerva-lex-stale_any}. So the configurations the plugins ran at the time were strong on conversations and not on code facts: the numbers in the first paragraphs of this section need the MiniLM embedder.

\paragraph{Mem0.} On a random \V{mem0n}-question development sample of LongMemEval-S, Mem0's verbatim mode reached \V{mem0-mem0-1024} at 1024 tokens against \V{mem0-first_version-1024} for the first version of the method and \V{mem0-hybrid-1024} for hybrid on the same questions, with \V{mem0add} ms per write. Mem0's LLM extraction, its main feature, was not exercised.

\subsection{Staleness in the first test}\label{sec:stale}
Table~\ref{tab:stale} reports, on RepoMem queries whose fact changed, how often an outdated version is \emph{injected}, and the \emph{error}: how often it is injected and the current version is absent or listed below it. Similarity retrieval injects the outdated version nearly always, \V{st-repomem-fresh-plain-hybrid-stale_any} of the time at 1024 tokens. With keys, exact version chains remove stale items entirely, and hybrid with invalidation does this as well as \kanerva. Without keys, \kanerva injects a stale version \V{st-repomem-fresh-plain-kanerva-stale_any} of the time, and the simple rule of keeping the newest near-twin reaches \V{st-repomem-fresh-plain-hybrid-dedup-stale_any}. So on RepoMem the gain comes from that rule, which \kanerva also uses, and not from its probabilistic validity model. We have no ablation that isolates the hazard model's value.

The rule depends on near-identical text, and RepoMem's versions differ only in a value. On the \emph{natural-language} updates of LongMemEval, evidence for a fact and for its update are not near-twins, the rule does not fire, and on development data \kanerva injects the outdated turn on \V{up-kanerva-inject} of questions against \V{up-hybrid-inject} for hybrid, since it finds more evidence of both kinds. Listing items newest first keeps the error to \V{up-kanerva-error}, against \V{up-hybrid-error} for hybrid in rank order. The store alone cannot tell that a free-text statement replaces an older one, since that needs semantics an LLM-free method lacks. A host agent has them, and Section~\ref{sec:writepath} measures one.

\subsection{Second held-out test, earlier version}\label{sec:second}
The first test showed that the near-twin rules treat similar events as versions of one fact. We changed one thing: an item can only be replaced by, or discounted against, another fact, so episodic notes such as commits never replace each other. Thresholds were tuned on the seventeen repositories seen so far, choosing the highest recall that did not raise stale injection, which kept them unchanged. The relevance model was not refitted. On BEAM all items are episodic, so the rule is inactive there, and the shipped method matches the variant without near-twin rules in Table~\ref{tab:freshablation}, a difference of \V{d-beam-kanerva_vs_-supersession-mean}.

Eight more repositories, named before their histories were fetched, form the test: uvicorn, anyio, trio, tox, waitress, gunicorn, alembic and jsonschema, with \V{n-repomem-new} queries. Eight units also make significance reachable. The method was frozen first, and the test ran once. Tables~\ref{tab:new} and~\ref{tab:newtests} give the results.

The change helps on its own: averaged over budgets it adds \V{nd-repomem-new-kanerva_vs_kanerva-events-mean} with keys and \V{nd-repomem-new-plain-kanerva_vs_kanerva-events-mean} without, with unit intervals \V{nci-repomem-new-kanerva_vs_kanerva-events-mean} and \V{nci-repomem-new-plain-kanerva_vs_kanerva-events-mean}. Without keys at 4096 tokens, task recall rises from \V{ntrack-repomem-new-plain-task-kanerva-events-4096} to \V{ntrack-repomem-new-plain-task-kanerva-4096}, against \V{ntrack-repomem-new-plain-task-hybrid-4096} for hybrid, and stale injection stays at \V{nst-repomem-new-plain-kanerva} against \V{nst-repomem-new-plain-kanerva-events} for the earlier rule and \V{nst-repomem-new-plain-hybrid} for hybrid.

With keys, the method now beats every rival: \V{nd-repomem-new-kanerva_vs_hybrid-mean} over hybrid, \V{nd-repomem-new-kanerva_vs_rerank-mean} over the reranker and \V{nd-repomem-new-kanerva_vs_hybrid-live-mean} over hybrid with invalidation, each with $p=$ \V{np-repomem-new-kanerva_vs_hybrid-mean} on the mean, and \V{npos-repomem-new-kanerva_vs_hybrid-mean} repositories favour it over hybrid. Without keys it does not: the difference to hybrid is \V{nd-repomem-new-plain-kanerva_vs_hybrid-mean} with interval \V{nci-repomem-new-plain-kanerva_vs_hybrid-mean}, and to the reranker \V{nd-repomem-new-plain-kanerva_vs_rerank-mean} with interval \V{nci-repomem-new-plain-kanerva_vs_rerank-mean}. It leads at 128 tokens and trails slightly at 1024. Part of that is the rule still firing on exact facts: fact recall at 4096 tokens is \V{ntrack-repomem-new-plain-fact-kanerva-4096} against \V{ntrack-repomem-new-plain-fact-hybrid-4096}.

The lighter configurations remain behind on code. The lexical model without keys is level with BM25 on average (\V{nd-repomem-new-plain-kanerva-lex_vs_bm25-mean}, interval \V{nci-repomem-new-plain-kanerva-lex_vs_bm25-mean}), and below it at 1024 tokens, on \V{npos-repomem-new-plain-kanerva-lex_vs_bm25-1024} repositories in its favour. The static embedder injects a stale version \V{nst-repomem-new-static-kanerva} of the time.

We tried to narrow that gap by comparing twins with hashed words, as the lexical model does, instead of the static embedder's vectors. Every RepoMem repository has been seen by now, so these are development numbers. A setting had to lower stale injection and keep mean recall on all four groups of repositories, and none of the \V{tw-settings} we tried did. The closest raised mean recall on each group, by \V{twh-repomem-new-diff} on the second test's repositories (interval \V{twh-repomem-new-ci}), but stale injection there rose from \V{tw-repomem-new-stale} to \V{twh-repomem-new-stale}. Stricter thresholds lowered it and lost recall. We kept the embedder's vectors.

\paragraph{Beyond Python.} RepoMem's task track needs only commit history, so unlike the fact track it can run on other languages. Nine active repositories, named before their histories were fetched, give \V{n-repomem-other} task queries: vite, hono and zod in TypeScript, gin, caddy and fzf in Go, ripgrep, axum and clap in Rust. Nothing was refitted, and the models had seen code only from Python repositories. Recall stays in the same range as on the Python task track, \V{olr-kanerva-1024} against \V{ntrack-repomem-new-plain-task-kanerva-1024} at 1024 tokens, so the method does not break. Its advantage does not carry over, though. On the second test's task track it led hybrid search by \V{ol-python-lead-1024} at 1024 tokens. Here it is level over all budgets (\V{old-kanerva_vs_hybrid-mean}, interval \V{olci-kanerva_vs_hybrid-mean}, \V{olpos-kanerva_vs_hybrid-mean} repositories in its favour) and behind at 128 and 256 tokens (\V{old-kanerva_vs_hybrid-128}, interval \V{olci-kanerva_vs_hybrid-128}, and \V{old-kanerva_vs_hybrid-256}, interval \V{olci-kanerva_vs_hybrid-256}). It is level with the reranker (\V{old-kanerva_vs_rerank-mean}, interval \V{olci-kanerva_vs_rerank-mean}), and the lexical model is level with BM25 (\V{old-kanerva-lex_vs_bm25-mean}, interval \V{olci-kanerva-lex_vs_bm25-mean}). With nine units these intervals are wide, and the check says nothing about exact code facts in these languages.

\subsection{Third held-out test, version 0.5.0}\label{sec:third}
\paragraph{What changed.} Against version 0.4.1 the retrieval path has four changes. The relevance models were refitted on all 34 RepoMem repositories, keyed and unkeyed, and on the conversation sets, with chats and code as two families of equal weight. A newer near-twin now replaces an older note only if it covers the query at least as well. Twins compared by hashed words have their own removal threshold of 0.85. And the plugins have a query gate, measured on its own in Section~\ref{sec:gate}. All of this was tuned on repositories the first two tests had used, so those tests cannot judge it. The static model2vec model was not refitted and is not part of this test.

The training recipe was picked by leave-repository-out recall. Scored out of fold over all budgets, the new recipe moves mean recall from \V{refit-dense-as-shipped-repomem-other} to \V{refit-dense-all-families-repomem-other} on the TypeScript, Go and Rust repositories, from \V{refit-dense-as-shipped-repomem-new-plain} to \V{refit-dense-all-families-repomem-new-plain} on the second test's repositories without keys, and from \V{refit-dense-as-shipped-lme} to \V{refit-dense-all-families-lme} on LongMemEval-S. It costs a little on LoCoMo, from \V{refit-dense-as-shipped-locomo} to \V{refit-dense-all-families-locomo}. The lexical model moves from \V{refit-lexical-as-shipped-repomem-other} to \V{refit-lexical-all-families-repomem-other} on the same three languages and stays at \V{refit-lexical-all-families-locomo} on LoCoMo.

\paragraph{The test.} Fifteen repositories that no fit, threshold or design choice had used, with \V{n-repomem-final} queries over three seeded streams each: scrapy, celery, httpie, sanic, boto3 and channels in Python, vue core, TanStack query and immer in TypeScript, cobra, echo and bubbletea in Go, bat, hyper and fd in Rust. The method was frozen first and the test ran once, so nothing here was tuned on its results. Code review afterwards fixed defects in the plugins and in one experiment script, and a rerun of sampled rows matched the stored results exactly. The fact track reads Python source, so it exists only for the six Python repositories, and the other nine have task queries alone. With fifteen units, significance is reachable. \texttt{kanerva-0.4} and \texttt{kanerva-lex-0.4} are the released 0.4.1 method, with its model and rules. Tables~\ref{tab:final} and~\ref{tab:finaltests} give the results, and Figure~\ref{fig:recall} shows recall by budget.

\paragraph{Recall.} Without keys, averaged over budgets, \kanerva has \R{repomem-final-plain}{kanerva}{mean} against \R{repomem-final-plain}{hybrid}{mean} for hybrid and \R{repomem-final-plain}{rerank}{mean} for the reranker. That is \V{fd-repomem-final-plain-kanerva_vs_hybrid-mean} over hybrid, unit interval \V{fci-repomem-final-plain-kanerva_vs_hybrid-mean}, with \V{fpos-repomem-final-plain-kanerva_vs_hybrid-mean} repositories in its favour at $p$ \V{fp-repomem-final-plain-kanerva_vs_hybrid-mean}, and \V{fd-repomem-final-plain-kanerva_vs_rerank-mean} over the reranker, interval \V{fci-repomem-final-plain-kanerva_vs_rerank-mean}. It leads at every budget. With keys the difference to hybrid is \V{fd-repomem-final-kanerva_vs_hybrid-mean}, interval \V{fci-repomem-final-kanerva_vs_hybrid-mean}, and to hybrid with invalidation \V{fd-repomem-final-kanerva_vs_hybrid-live-mean}, interval \V{fci-repomem-final-kanerva_vs_hybrid-live-mean}. Unlike in the second test, the lead no longer depends on keys. Keys can matter only on the six repositories with facts, and there the lead is much the same with them and without them.

Against the released 0.4.1 the gain is \V{fd-repomem-final-plain-kanerva_vs_kanerva-0.4-mean} without keys, interval \V{fci-repomem-final-plain-kanerva_vs_kanerva-0.4-mean}, and \V{fd-repomem-final-kanerva_vs_kanerva-0.4-mean} with keys, interval \V{fci-repomem-final-kanerva_vs_kanerva-0.4-mean}. The query condition on near-twins accounts for \V{fd-repomem-final-plain-kanerva_vs_kanerva-any-twin-mean} of the first figure, interval \V{fci-repomem-final-plain-kanerva_vs_kanerva-any-twin-mean}. It moves nothing with keys, where twins are not used, and it can act only on the six repositories with facts, which is why it shows \V{fpos-repomem-final-plain-kanerva_vs_kanerva-any-twin-mean}. Without keys at 1024 tokens, task recall is \V{ftrack-repomem-final-plain-task-kanerva-1024} against \V{ftrack-repomem-final-plain-task-hybrid-1024} for hybrid. Fact recall is \V{ftrack-repomem-final-plain-fact-kanerva-1024} against \V{ftrack-repomem-final-plain-fact-hybrid-1024}, so the method still gives up a little on exact facts.

\paragraph{Lexical.} This is what a store runs with \texttt{KANERVA\_EMBEDDER=off} or until the model has arrived. It stays close to BM25. Averaged over budgets without keys it has \R{repomem-final-plain}{kanerva-lex}{mean} against \R{repomem-final-plain}{bm25}{mean}, a difference of \V{fd-repomem-final-plain-kanerva-lex_vs_bm25-mean} with interval \V{fci-repomem-final-plain-kanerva-lex_vs_bm25-mean} and \V{fpos-repomem-final-plain-kanerva-lex_vs_bm25-mean} repositories in its favour. \subsection{Real prompts}\label{sec:realprompts}
Every prompt above comes from a template or a dataset. Here we replay one developer's own Claude Code transcripts through the hooks, project by project and in time order, into throwaway stores: \V{rs-prompts} prompts in \V{rs-sessions} sessions over \V{rs-projects} projects, \V{rs-vectors-ru-n} of them in Russian. Prompts from another project stand in for prompts a store cannot answer. A store holds what the hooks write, and where the wrap-up rule asks for a note, the turn's last reply stands in for it. Prompts stayed on the machine and the results hold counts only. This is a small sample of one person's work, and anyone can run the script on their own transcripts and get other numbers.

The gate separates much less here than on the benchmarks. With vectors, own prompts get notes \V{rs-vectors-all-free_spoke}\% of the time without it and \V{rs-vectors-all-gated_spoke}\% with it, and prompts from other projects \V{rs-vectors-foreign-all-free_spoke}\% and \V{rs-vectors-foreign-all-gated_spoke}\%. Lexical stores go from \V{rs-lexical-all-free_spoke}\% to \V{rs-lexical-all-gated_spoke}\% on own prompts and from \V{rs-lexical-foreign-all-free_spoke}\% to \V{rs-lexical-foreign-all-gated_spoke}\% on foreign ones, so there it barely separates them. Language matters: with vectors, English own prompts get notes \V{rs-vectors-en-gated_spoke}\% of the time with the gate, and Russian ones \V{rs-vectors-ru-gated_spoke}\%, because these notes are mostly in English. The gate still halves the noise, at a real cost in silence, and its false-alarm rates in Table~\ref{tab:gate} do not carry over to real use.

A weak label says when a store could have helped: one of its notes names a file that this turn then touched, which holds for \V{rs-vectors-all-known} prompts. On those the gate speaks \V{rs-vectors-all-known_gated_spoke}\% of the time, against \V{rs-vectors-all-known_free_spoke}\% without it, but the notes that a prompt brings name that file only \V{rs-vectors-all-known_gated_hit}\% of the time. So a real prompt rarely finds the note about the file the agent will open, which is the case for file notes.

These sessions never had these notes, so an agent cannot have used them, and each time the use signal of Section~\ref{sec:online} fires here it is wrong. It fires on \V{rs-vectors-fired}\% of the notes a prompt brought, and on \V{rs-vectors-random}\% of random notes from the same store. A signal that fires this often on notes nobody saw cannot tell use from coincidence, so learning stays opt-in. The same replay showed \V{rs-vectors-edit-lines}\% of the lines at session start to be lists of edited files, which only repeat the git log, so the plugins now leave them out.

\subsection{The write path without keys}\label{sec:writepath}
A note without a key replaces an older one only if someone says so, so \texttt{memory\_remember} lists similar facts and the host agent decides (Section~\ref{sec:method}). We replay streams with no keys and ask for this list as each note is written. No LLM takes part. Conversation turns are replayed as facts, since an agent's notes are facts. The word thresholds were picked on development streams, the cosine threshold comes from the real agent below, and Table~\ref{tab:writepath} reports streams they were not picked on.

On the third test with vectors, the older version of a changed fact comes first on \V{wp-vectors-repomem-final-top1} of changed notes, and it is on the list on \V{wp-vectors-repomem-final-top3}. Lexically the figures are \V{wp-lexical-repomem-final-top1} and \V{wp-lexical-repomem-final-top3}. Natural language is much harder: on LongMemEval updates they fall to \V{wp-vectors-lme-updates-top1} and \V{wp-vectors-lme-updates-top3}, on only \V{wp-vectors-lme-updates-changed} changed notes.

The list has many false alarms. On the third test it appears on \V{wp-vectors-repomem-final-shown_on_other_notes-p}\% of the notes that replace nothing, and across all lists the top hit is something other than an older version \V{wp-vectors-repomem-final-top_hit_not_older-p}\% of the time. On LongMemEval the rates are \V{wp-vectors-lme-updates-shown_on_other_notes-p}\% and \V{wp-vectors-lme-updates-top_hit_not_older-p}\%. Each list costs about \V{wp-vectors-repomem-final-tokens} tokens of the agent's context. So an agent that replaced whatever came first would retire good notes, and the value of the list depends on the agent's judgement.

The stale columns are a simulated upper bound. An oracle stands in for the host agent and marks the true older version whenever the list shows it. Stale injection on the third test's changed facts then falls from \V{wp-vectors-repomem-final-current_rule-stale-p}\% to \V{wp-vectors-repomem-final-oracle_simulated-stale-p}\% with vectors, and from \V{wp-lexical-repomem-final-current_rule-stale-p}\% to \V{wp-lexical-repomem-final-oracle_simulated-stale-p}\% lexically. On LongMemEval updates it falls from \V{wp-vectors-lme-updates-current_rule-stale-p}\% to \V{wp-vectors-lme-updates-oracle_simulated-stale-p}\%. These numbers say what a perfect agent could reach.

\paragraph{A real agent.} Here Claude Code with the plugin and \V{awp-model} decides for itself. Each trial gets a store replayed up to a note and an instruction to remember it, and the store afterwards shows what the agent did. A \emph{verbatim} note keeps a text word for word. An \emph{own} note is what an agent normally writes: a first session turns the older conversation turn into a short note in its own words, and a second, fresh session does the same with the newer turn.

The first round failed on conversations. On the third test's changed facts the agent retired the older version \V{awpfirst-repomem-final-changed-replaced} times out of \V{awpfirst-repomem-final-changed-n}, and on LongMemEval updates \V{awpfirst-lme-updates-changed-replaced} times out of \V{awpfirst-lme-updates-changed-n}. Its replies gave the reasons, round by round. A conversation turn holds several subjects, so it cannot be retired whole, and the hint now says to take the outdated part out with \texttt{memory\_edit}. An instruction inside a tool result did not count as a request from the user, so the server's instructions now say that keeping memory current is part of saving. Paraphrases of one fact missed a cosine threshold of 0.95 that came from word-for-word data, so it is now 0.3, in a list of five that alternates word matches and close vectors. A list showed the opening of a long note, while the outdated detail sat further on, so it now shows the sentence closest to the new note. A personal note saved as global saw only global notes, so a note now sees both. And the agent left an old value alone as a dated snapshot, so an edit now keeps the earlier text as history, and the instructions say that notes state what is true now while a dated event stays. We also dropped six LongMemEval questions that ask about the past, since there the older fact is the answer. In the last sample two notes on one fact sit from \V{awp-cos-changed-min} to \V{awp-cos-changed-max}, median \V{awp-cos-changed-median}, and notes on different subjects reach at most \V{awp-cos-other-max}.

\subsection{Russian and cross-language search}\label{sec:russian}
Russian support adds a Snowball stemmer, stopwords and query patterns, and \texttt{KANERVA\_EMBEDDER=multilingual} adds search across languages. They act on Cyrillic text or when asked for, so English behaviour is unchanged. We test on XQuAD \cite{artetxe2020xquad}, which gives SQuAD paragraphs and questions in English and in professional Russian translation: \V{ru-articles} articles, each a store of five paragraphs, with \V{ru-questions} questions, and one pooled store of all paragraphs. A retrieval counts if it returns the question's paragraph, and the unit is the article. This is not coding-agent memory, and the relevance model has not seen Russian.

Stemming helps once a note fits. With article stores it moves lexical recall from \V{ru-article-ru-ru-stem-off-1024} to \V{ru-article-ru-ru-stem-on-1024} at 1024 tokens, a difference of \V{rud-article_ru-ru_stem-on_vs_stem-off-1024} with interval \V{ruci-article_ru-ru_stem-on_vs_stem-off-1024}, and in the pooled store from \V{ru-pooled-ru-ru-stem-off-1024} to \V{ru-pooled-ru-ru-stem-on-1024}. With excerpts, which are off by default, recall at 128 tokens is \V{ru-article-ru-ru-stem-on-excerpts-128}.

For a Russian question over English notes, lexical search finds almost nothing, \V{ru-pooled-ru-en-lexical-1024} in the pooled store at 1024 tokens. MiniLM does well inside a store of five paragraphs, at \V{ru-article-ru-en-minilm-1024}, but fails in the pooled store, at \V{ru-pooled-ru-en-minilm-1024}. The multilingual model reaches \V{ru-article-ru-en-multilingual-1024} and \V{ru-pooled-ru-en-multilingual-1024}. \subsection{Online learning}\label{sec:online}

\subsection{Answers from a local model}\label{sec:answers}
Recall says what reaches the context and not what a model does with it, so here a small local model answers each question from the notes a method selected. The memory layer still calls no LLM. The reader is Qwen3-4B in 4-bit form on MLX, pinned by revision, and the prompt tells it to answer from the notes alone, in as few words as it can, or to say unknown. We take \V{qan-locomo} questions from LoCoMo, \V{qan-lme} from LongMemEval-S, \V{qan-beam-dev} from BEAM-1M and \V{qan-repomem-final} fact queries from the six Python repositories of the third test, without keys, at 256, 512 and 1024 tokens. The first three are development data. The fourth is the only set here that no decision used, and with six repositories the smallest possible two-sided $p$ is \V{qa-min-p}.

Only short extractive answers can be scored without a judge. LoCoMo keeps categories 1 and 4, LongMemEval-S drops temporal reasoning and preferences, and BEAM keeps knowledge updates. Two categories went after a pilot because the reader missed them even when handed the labelled notes, and no method comparison informed that. Scoring rules were fixed on small pilot runs before the full run. The score counts an answer as right if it has every word of the reference, in any order, or for code the whole value as an unbroken run. That is strict: a reply of ``four months'' fails against ``about four months''. With no notes the score is zero on every set. The last row of Table~\ref{tab:answers} hands the reader the labelled evidence alone. It is a reference and not a bound, and on BEAM-1M some methods pass it.

\paragraph{Methods.} Across every comparison and metric, \V{qa-raw} of \V{qa-tests} tests reach raw $p<0.05$ and \V{qa-holm} survive Holm correction, so this test shows no reliable difference between methods. Of the \V{qa-means} means in Table~\ref{tab:answertests}, \V{qa-means-raw} reaches raw $p<0.05$: on the third test's facts \kanerva beats BM25 by \V{qad-repomem-final-kanerva_vs_bm25-mean} points, interval \V{qaci-repomem-final-kanerva_vs_bm25-mean}, on all six repositories. One such result in that many comparisons is about what chance gives. The intervals are wide, so a null here does not mean two methods are equal.

\paragraph{Recall against answers.} On conversations recall barely converts. \subsection{Live sessions}\label{sec:live}
We also ran the plugin in real Claude Code sessions with \V{awp-model}, on small Python and C++ projects with an isolated store. Memory carried over: after one session found that the machine has no \texttt{python}, the next went straight to \texttt{python3}, and after a C++ session learned that the tests need a Debug build for their assertions, the next ran that CMake command first. No secret reached the store when a session was told to remember a token, although the agent wrote it to the host's own memory files.

These sessions found defects no benchmark could. Notes held absolute paths. Edits to the host's own memory files counted as work and became notes. A test command that was never found was kept as the project's test command. File notes repeated what the prompt had just brought. Requests to remember something went to the host's own memory and not to the store, until the server's instructions said otherwise, after which three of three went to the store. And in \texttt{claude -p} the wrap-up turn replaced the message a script reads, so it is now off in that mode. All of these are fixed. These runs are anecdotes, not measurements.

\subsection{Excerpts}
Turn recall credits an excerpt that lost the answer, so excerpts are compared on \emph{answer-term recall}: the share of the reference answer's content stems, excluding those in the question, found in the injected text. In the BEAM run of the first test, at 128, 256 and 512 tokens, excerpts of long items raise it from \V{ans-beam-kanerva-128}, \V{ans-beam-kanerva-256} and \V{ans-beam-kanerva-512} to \V{ans-beam-kanerva-x128-128}, \V{ans-beam-kanerva-x128-256} and \V{ans-beam-kanerva-x128-512}. \subsection{Checks of the theory on synthetic instances}
With known ground truth (eight seeds, horizon 4000, 40 items per round, three classes with different hazards), cumulative regret against the per-round greedy optimum is \V{regret-known} for a learner that knows validity, \V{regret-estimated} when it estimates hazards from censored lifetimes, and \V{regret-blind} when it is blind. The log--log slopes in $T$ are \V{slope-known}, \V{slope-estimated} and \V{slope-blind}. The blind learner's linear growth is what Proposition~\ref{prop:blind} predicts, and it shows the value of the separation. The observed regret of the other two is far below the upper bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:regret}, whose value here exceeds the trivial bound, so these runs do not test the bound's tightness. Over \V{certn} random selection problems, $F(S)$ was on average \V{certmean} of the certificate upper bound, with a minimum of \V{certmin}, above the worst-case constant of $0.316$.

\subsection{Latency and scale}
On an M1 Pro with an uncontended CPU, a fresh query at $10^4$ items takes \V{p50-hybrid@10000} ms at the median and \V{p95-hybrid@10000} ms at the 95th percentile, plus \V{encode} ms to embed the query, and lexical retrieval takes \V{p50-lexical@10000} and \V{p95-lexical@10000} ms. At $10^5$ items the medians are \V{p50-hybrid@100000} and \V{p50-lexical@100000} ms. A write costs \V{wr-hybrid@10000} ms, excluding the embedding. These timings come from the earlier version. In a plugin, a hook starts a new process each time, so one prompt at $10^4$ items takes \V{hook-minilm@10000} s in all with MiniLM, \V{hook-lexical@10000} s lexical and \V{hook-static@10000} s with the static embedder. The memory layer spends no LLM tokens, so its token cost is the injected context alone.

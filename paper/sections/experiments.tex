\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}
\paragraph{Mode.} All metrics are retrieval-level: no LLM takes part in the method or in the evaluation, so we report what reaches the context and not what a model does with it.

\paragraph{Development data.} \emph{LongMemEval-S} \cite{wu2025longmemeval} has 500 questions, each with its own store of about 490 turns. \emph{LoCoMo} \cite{maharana2024locomo} has 10 long conversations. \emph{BEAM-1M} \cite{tavakoli2026beam} has 35 long conversations of about 2,000 turns. \emph{RepoMem} (new) turns eight Python repositories into memory streams, at 14 snapshots over six years. Each interval is a session in which a simulated agent records commit notes and facts observed in changed files. Facts (constants, signatures, symbol locations, dependency constraints) come from \texttt{ast}, so a fact is stale exactly when the same key held another value at an earlier snapshot. Fact queries ask for the current value; task queries use a real later commit subject, with earlier commits on the same files as gold. RepoMem runs with slot keys, as a host agent would supply, and without.

\paragraph{Held-out test.} \emph{BEAM-100K and BEAM-500K}: \V{n-beam} questions in 55 conversations that share no seed with BEAM-1M, keeping every question with labelled evidence. Their turns average \V{goldtok-beam} tokens for evidence and \V{othertok-beam} for the rest, and the full history of a conversation averages \V{full-beam} tokens. \emph{RepoMem} on five repositories outside development (marshmallow, pluggy, packaging, loguru, tqdm): \V{n-repomem-fresh} queries over three seeded streams each. No model, threshold or design choice was made on this data. The method was frozen first, and the test ran once, with a single script.

\paragraph{Baselines.} Recency, BM25 (FTS5), dense (all-MiniLM-L6-v2 \cite{hf_minilm}), hybrid RRF \cite{cormack2009rrf}, hybrid followed by a cross-encoder (ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2) on the top 50, MMR \cite{carbonell1998mmr}, Generative Agents scoring \cite{park2023generative} with constant importance, facility location under a knapsack as in PACMS \cite{pacms2026}, hybrid with superseded versions removed (temporal invalidation alone, in the spirit of \cite{rasmussen2025zep}), hybrid listed newest first, and hybrid keeping only the newest of near-twins. We also keep the \emph{first version} of the method: a linear prior over retrieval signals with no item, role or context features, and a pool of 100 from each retriever. All methods share candidate generators, token counts (\texttt{cl100k\_base}) and a 14-token per-item overhead, and every ranking is filled greedily to the budget, which already makes the baselines budget-aware. Systems that need an LLM on write were not run.

\paragraph{Statistics.} The independent unit is a conversation on BEAM (55) and a repository on RepoMem (5), since queries within a conversation are dependent and seeds repeat RepoMem queries. We give bootstrap intervals on the unit, and $p$-values from two-sided sign-flip permutation tests on the unit means, with Holm correction across budgets within a comparison. With five units the smallest possible two-sided $p$ is $1/16$, so no RepoMem comparison can reach significance. There, read the interval and the count of units favouring each side, and note that an interval containing zero on five units is weak evidence that two methods are equal.

\subsection{Development results}
Table~\ref{tab:dev} gives cross-fitted recall: each query is scored by a model that never saw its question, conversation or repository. The relevance model raises mean recall over the first version on all four datasets, from \V{dev-lme-first-version-mean} to \V{dev-lme-kanerva-mean} on LongMemEval-S, \V{dev-locomo-first-version-mean} to \V{dev-locomo-kanerva-mean} on LoCoMo, \V{dev-repomem-first-version-mean} to \V{dev-repomem-kanerva-mean} on RepoMem and \V{dev-beam-dev-first-version-mean} to \V{dev-beam-dev-kanerva-mean} on BEAM-1M. The large-budget loss to hybrid ranking disappears on LoCoMo: \V{dev-locomo-kanerva-4096} against \V{dev-locomo-hybrid-4096} at 4096 tokens. Without a cross-encoder the method still trails the reranker at the smallest budget, \V{dev-locomo-kanerva-128} against \V{dev-locomo-rerank-128} on LoCoMo at 128 tokens, because at that size one item decides everything and only joint reading of query and text separates the top candidates. With cross-encoder features it closes that gap, at \V{dev-locomo-kanerva-ce-128}.

The candidate pool matters most on BEAM, where a question has many evidence turns. There the top 100 from each retriever reach \V{pool-beam-dev-pool100} of the evidence, and 200 reach \V{pool-beam-dev-pool200}, at a cost of \V{pool-beam-dev-size200} candidates instead of \V{pool-beam-dev-size100}. Adding the neighbours of the 50 best hits reaches \V{pool-beam-dev-nb50x1} with \V{pool-beam-dev-nb50x1_size} candidates, so neighbours add about three times more evidence per candidate than a deeper pool, while pseudo-relevance feedback adds little (\V{pool-beam-dev-prf}).

Table~\ref{tab:devablation} removes one feature group at a time and refits. Item features and neighbourhood features carry most of the gain, and query--item overlap adds little on top of them.

With calibrated weights, the selection rule hardly matters: over the four datasets, mean recall is \V{rule-lme-density} for density greedy against \V{rule-lme-dp} for an exact knapsack on LongMemEval-S, and \V{rule-beam-dev-density} against \V{rule-beam-dev-dp} on BEAM-1M. We keep density greedy because Theorem~\ref{thm:selection} covers it.

\subsection{Held-out test}
Figure~\ref{fig:recall} and Table~\ref{tab:fresh} give the results, and Table~\ref{tab:freshtests} the paired differences.

\paragraph{Long conversations.} On BEAM the method leads every rival, and the lead grows with the budget. Averaged over budgets it has \R{beam}{kanerva}{mean} against \R{beam}{rerank}{mean} for the reranker and \R{beam}{hybrid}{mean} for hybrid, a difference of \V{d-beam-kanerva_vs_rerank-mean} over the reranker with unit interval \V{ci-beam-kanerva_vs_rerank-mean}, and \V{pos-beam-kanerva_vs_rerank-mean} conversations favour it. At 1024 tokens recall is \R{beam}{kanerva}{1024} against \R{beam}{rerank}{1024}. Below that the gap narrows, and at 128 tokens the two are level, at \R{beam}{kanerva}{128} and \R{beam}{rerank}{128}. Adding cross-encoder features gives \R{beam}{kanerva-ce}{128} at 128 tokens and \R{beam}{kanerva-ce}{1024} at 1024. The gain over the first version is \V{d-beam-kanerva_vs_kanerva-r1-mean} with interval \V{ci-beam-kanerva_vs_kanerva-r1-mean}. Removing the wide pool and neighbours costs \V{d-beam-kanerva_vs_-neighbours-mean}, and removing cost density costs \V{d-beam-kanerva_vs_-density-mean} (Table~\ref{tab:freshablation}).

\paragraph{Code history.} On RepoMem the picture is mixed. The method leads at the smallest budget, with keys \R{repomem-fresh}{kanerva}{128} against \R{repomem-fresh}{rerank}{128} for the reranker and \R{repomem-fresh}{hybrid-live}{128} for hybrid with invalidation. From 1024 tokens it falls behind: at 4096 tokens with keys it has \R{repomem-fresh}{kanerva}{4096} against \R{repomem-fresh}{hybrid-live}{4096}, and without keys \R{repomem-fresh-plain}{kanerva}{4096} against \R{repomem-fresh-plain}{hybrid}{4096}. Averaged over budgets the difference to hybrid is \V{d-repomem-fresh-kanerva_vs_hybrid-mean} with keys and \V{d-repomem-fresh-plain-kanerva_vs_hybrid-mean} without, with intervals \V{ci-repomem-fresh-kanerva_vs_hybrid-mean} and \V{ci-repomem-fresh-plain-kanerva_vs_hybrid-mean} that contain zero on five repositories. The first version is beaten on every repository, by \V{d-repomem-fresh-plain-kanerva_vs_kanerva-r1-mean} without keys. The loss at large budgets sits entirely on the task track, and it comes from the near-twin rules, which take look-alike commit notes for versions of one fact. Without keys at 4096 tokens, task recall is \V{track-repomem-fresh-plain-task-kanerva-4096}, against \V{track-repomem-fresh-plain-task--supersession-4096} with those rules removed and \V{track-repomem-fresh-plain-task-hybrid-4096} for hybrid, while fact recall is \V{track-repomem-fresh-plain-fact-kanerva-4096}. Removing the rules altogether would bring back the stale injections shown next, so restricting them to facts is the obvious change, which we did not test.

\paragraph{Lighter configurations.} The lexical model needs no embeddings and is the plugin default. On BEAM it has \R{beam}{kanerva-lex}{mean} against \R{beam}{bm25}{mean} for BM25, on the same candidates. On RepoMem without keys it has \R{repomem-fresh-plain}{kanerva-lex}{mean} against \R{repomem-fresh-plain}{bm25}{mean}, and it loses to BM25 at 4096 tokens, \R{repomem-fresh-plain}{kanerva-lex}{4096} against \R{repomem-fresh-plain}{bm25}{4096}. With the static model2vec embedder \cite{model2vec}, which needs no neural runtime, the method reaches \R{beam-static}{kanerva}{mean} on BEAM and \R{repomem-fresh-static}{kanerva}{mean} on RepoMem.

\paragraph{Mem0.} On a random \V{mem0n}-question development sample of LongMemEval-S, Mem0's verbatim mode reached \V{mem0-mem0-1024} at 1024 tokens against \V{mem0-first_version-1024} for the first version of the method and \V{mem0-hybrid-1024} for hybrid on the same questions, with \V{mem0add} ms per write. Mem0's LLM extraction, its main feature, was not exercised.

\subsection{Staleness}\label{sec:stale}
Table~\ref{tab:stale} reports, on RepoMem queries whose fact changed, how often an outdated version is \emph{injected}, and the \emph{error}: how often it is injected and the current version is absent or listed below it. Similarity retrieval injects the outdated version nearly always, \V{st-repomem-fresh-plain-hybrid-stale_any} of the time at 1024 tokens. With keys, exact version chains remove stale items entirely, and hybrid with invalidation does this as well as \kanerva. Without keys, \kanerva injects a stale version \V{st-repomem-fresh-plain-kanerva-stale_any} of the time, and the simple rule of keeping the newest near-twin reaches \V{st-repomem-fresh-plain-hybrid-dedup-stale_any}. So on RepoMem the gain comes from that rule, which \kanerva also uses, and not from its probabilistic validity model. We have no ablation that isolates the hazard model's value.

The rule depends on near-identical text, and RepoMem's versions differ only in a value. On the \emph{natural-language} updates of LongMemEval, evidence for a fact and for its update are not near-twins, the rule does not fire, and on development data \kanerva injects the outdated turn on \V{up-kanerva-inject} of questions against \V{up-hybrid-inject} for hybrid, since it finds more evidence of both kinds. Listing items newest first keeps the error to \V{up-kanerva-error}, against \V{up-hybrid-error} for hybrid in rank order. Detecting that a free-text statement replaces an older one remains unsolved here, and it likely needs semantics that an LLM-free method lacks.

\subsection{Excerpts}
Turn recall credits an excerpt that lost the answer, so excerpts are compared on \emph{answer-term recall}: the share of the reference answer's content stems, excluding those in the question, found in the injected text. On BEAM at 128, 256 and 512 tokens, excerpts of long items raise it from \V{ans-beam-kanerva-128}, \V{ans-beam-kanerva-256} and \V{ans-beam-kanerva-512} to \V{ans-beam-kanerva-x128-128}, \V{ans-beam-kanerva-x128-256} and \V{ans-beam-kanerva-x128-512}. The differences are small, \V{d-beam-kanerva-x128_vs_kanerva_(answer)-128} to \V{d-beam-kanerva-x128_vs_kanerva_(answer)-512}, and they fade by 1024 tokens, so the mode stays off by default.

\subsection{Online learning}
Feedback here is simulated from evidence labels, as described in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}. Each seed shuffles the order of work and shares a single policy. Table~\ref{tab:online} reports recall at $B=512$. On development data, where the model already fits well, online learning does not help. On LoCoMo recall moves from \V{on-locomo-static} to \V{on-locomo-online}, and on RepoMem from \V{on-repomem-static} to \V{on-repomem-online}, and LongMemEval-S gives each question its own store, so nothing carries over. Past use of an item for another query turns out to be a poor predictor for the next one. With 20\% of rewards flipped, recall on LoCoMo is \V{on-locomo-online-noisy}. So policy learning stays opt-in.

\subsection{Checks of the theory on synthetic instances}
With known ground truth (eight seeds, horizon 4000, 40 items per round, three classes with different hazards), cumulative regret against the per-round greedy optimum is \V{regret-known} for a learner that knows validity, \V{regret-estimated} when it estimates hazards from censored lifetimes, and \V{regret-blind} when it is blind. The log--log slopes in $T$ are \V{slope-known}, \V{slope-estimated} and \V{slope-blind}. The blind learner's linear growth is what Proposition~\ref{prop:blind} predicts, and it shows the value of the separation. The observed regret of the other two is far below the upper bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:regret}, whose value here exceeds the trivial bound, so these runs do not test the bound's tightness. Over \V{certn} random selection problems, $F(S)$ was on average \V{certmean} of the certificate upper bound, with a minimum of \V{certmin}, above the worst-case constant of $0.316$.

\subsection{Latency and scale}
On an M1 Pro with an uncontended CPU, a fresh query at $10^4$ items takes \V{p50-hybrid@10000} ms at the median and \V{p95-hybrid@10000} ms at the 95th percentile, plus \V{encode} ms to embed the query, and lexical retrieval takes \V{p50-lexical@10000} and \V{p95-lexical@10000} ms. At $10^5$ items the medians are \V{p50-hybrid@100000} and \V{p50-lexical@100000} ms. A write costs \V{wr-hybrid@10000} ms, excluding the embedding. The memory layer spends no LLM tokens, so its token cost is the injected context alone.

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}
\paragraph{Mode.} No LLM was available, so all metrics are retrieval-level. Hypotheses about end-to-end effects are out of scope.

\paragraph{Benchmarks.} \emph{LongMemEval-S} \cite{wu2025longmemeval}: 500 questions, each with its own store of about 490 turns; \V{n-lme} test questions remain after dropping those without flagged evidence and the dev split. \emph{LoCoMo} \cite{maharana2024locomo}: 10 long conversations; conversations 0--2 are dev, leaving \V{n-locomo} test questions. \emph{RepoMem} (new): eight Python repositories at 14 snapshots over six years. Each interval is a session in which the simulated agent records commit notes and facts observed in changed files. Facts (constants, signatures, symbol locations, dependency constraints) come from \texttt{ast}, so a fact is stale exactly when the same key held another value at an earlier snapshot. Fact queries ask for the current value; task queries use a real later commit subject, with earlier commits on the same files as gold. Two repositories are dev, six test (\V{n-repomem-keyed} queries over three seeded streams each). We run RepoMem with slot keys, as a host agent would supply, and without. Four more repositories are a holdout (Section~\ref{sec:posthoc}). A fourth track, \emph{LongMemEval updates}, takes the \V{nchanged-lme-updates} knowledge-update test questions with evidence in more than one session; the latest session's evidence is gold and earlier evidence is the outdated fact, in natural language.

\paragraph{Baselines.} No memory (recall $0$), full history (recall $1$ at the cost of the whole store, a mean of \V{full-lme} tokens per question on LongMemEval-S and \V{full-locomo} on LoCoMo), recency, BM25 (FTS5), dense (all-MiniLM-L6-v2 \cite{hf_minilm}), hybrid RRF \cite{cormack2009rrf}, hybrid followed by a cross-encoder reranker, MMR \cite{carbonell1998mmr}, Generative Agents scoring \cite{park2023generative} with constant importance, facility location under a knapsack as in PACMS \cite{pacms2026}, hybrid top-$k$ for $k\in\{3,5,10,20\}$, hybrid with superseded versions removed (temporal invalidation alone, in the spirit of \cite{rasmussen2025zep}), hybrid listed newest first, and hybrid keeping only the newest of near-twins. All methods share candidate generators, token counts (\texttt{cl100k\_base}) and a 14-token per-item overhead, the measured cost of an item's rendered prefix, and every ranking is filled greedily to the budget, which already makes the baselines budget-aware. Mem0 2.2.1 with \texttt{infer=False} and local Qdrant ran on a random \V{mem0n}-question sample. Systems that need an LLM on write were not run.

\paragraph{Protocol.} The prior $\theta_0$ and hyperparameters were set on dev splits only. Metrics are evidence recall, tokens injected, staleness (Section~\ref{sec:stale}), latency and store size. Retrieval is deterministic; seeds govern RepoMem streams and online runs (five seeds). The independent unit is a question on LongMemEval, a conversation on LoCoMo (seven) and a repository on RepoMem (six), since seeds repeat many RepoMem queries and queries within a conversation are dependent. We give bootstrap intervals on the unit, and query-level intervals as well, which are optimistic for clustered data. $p$-values are two-sided sign-flip permutation tests on the mean at the unit level, with Holm correction across rivals and budgets for each reference method. With six units, the smallest possible $p$ is $1/32$, so no RepoMem comparison can survive correction; there, read the unit interval and the count of units favoring each side.

\subsection{Recall against tokens}
Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} shows the fronts and Table~\ref{tab:recall} the values. \kanerva here uses only BM25 and a small embedding model, and the recall tables use the static prior with no learning. On LongMemEval-S it leads hybrid and BM25 by a wide margin in the middle of the range: at 1024 tokens recall is \R{lme}{kanerva}{1024} against \R{lme}{hybrid}{1024} and \R{lme}{bm25}{1024}. Most of that comes from cost density, and without it recall falls to \R{lme}{-density}{1024}. This partly reflects the dataset: evidence turns average \V{goldtok-lme} tokens and other turns \V{othertok-lme}, since evidence is mostly in short user turns, so density favors the right items. On LoCoMo the relation runs the other way, \V{goldtok-locomo} against \V{othertok-locomo} tokens, and density hurts slightly. There \kanerva leads hybrid up to 1024 tokens (\R{locomo}{kanerva}{1024} against \R{locomo}{hybrid}{1024}), is level with BM25 at small budgets, and loses to hybrid at large ones (\R{locomo}{kanerva}{4096} against \R{locomo}{hybrid}{4096} at 4096).

RepoMem fact queries are close to saturated: at 1024 tokens every method in Table~\ref{tab:category} is near or above 0.95 on them, BM25 included, so the differences between methods come from a small share of queries. There, \kanerva is level with the rivals or slightly behind: \R{repomem-plain}{kanerva}{1024} against \R{repomem-plain}{hybrid}{1024} for hybrid without keys, and \R{repomem-keyed}{kanerva}{1024} against \R{repomem-keyed}{hybrid-live}{1024} for hybrid with invalidation. Removing older near-twins also costs it on the task track, the only hard one, where events that look alike are not versions of one fact. On the holdout repositories without keys it has \R{repomem-holdout-plain}{kanerva}{1024} against \R{repomem-holdout-plain}{hybrid}{1024}.

The strongest rival is the cross-encoder reranker, which spends a neural model on every query (median \V{latce-lme} ms for 50 pairs on this machine's GPU). It beats \kanerva at small budgets: \R{locomo}{rerank}{128} against \R{locomo}{kanerva}{128} on LoCoMo at 128 tokens, and \R{lme}{rerank}{256} against \R{lme}{kanerva}{256} on LongMemEval-S at 256. By 1024 tokens the two are level within their intervals.

\paragraph{Extension with reranker features.} This result suggested feeding the cross-encoder's signals into the policy as three more features, with a prior refitted on dev. The variant was designed after seeing the test results above, so it is a post-hoc extension and needs confirming on fresh data. ``\kanerva + reranker'' narrows the gap (Table~\ref{tab:diffce}): against the plain reranker its unit-level intervals favor it at \V{cewin-lme-rerank} of six budgets on LongMemEval-S and \V{cewin-locomo-rerank} on LoCoMo, and favor the reranker at \V{celoss-lme-rerank} and \V{celoss-locomo-rerank}. It needs a model that the default plugin does not load.

\paragraph{Lighter configurations.} Embedding-free \kanerva, the plugin default, reaches \R{lme}{kanerva-lex}{1024} on LongMemEval-S and \R{locomo}{kanerva-lex}{1024} on LoCoMo at 1024 tokens. With the static model2vec embedder \cite{model2vec} in place of MiniLM these are \V{m2v-lme-kanerva} and \V{m2v-locomo-kanerva}.

\paragraph{Mem0.} On the \V{mem0n}-question sample, Mem0's verbatim mode reached \V{mem0-1024} at 1024 tokens against \V{mem0ours-1024} for \kanerva and \V{mem0hyb-1024} for hybrid on the same questions, with \V{mem0add} ms per write. Mem0's LLM extraction, its main feature, was not exercised.

\subsection{Staleness}\label{sec:stale}
Table~\ref{tab:stale} reports, on queries whose fact changed, how often an outdated version is \emph{injected}, and the \emph{error}: how often it is injected and the current version is absent or ranked below it. Injection is the stronger test. Listing hybrid's selection newest first drops its error from \Err{repomem-plain}{hybrid}{1024} to \Err{repomem-plain}{hybrid-newest}{1024} with no change to what is injected, \Inj{repomem-plain}{hybrid}{1024}.

The problem is real and large: similarity retrieval injects the outdated version nearly always on changed facts, and a larger budget does not help. The fix turns out to be cheap. With keys, exact version chains remove stale items entirely, and plain hybrid with invalidation does this as well as \kanerva, with slightly higher recall. Without keys, \kanerva injects a stale version on \Inj{repomem-plain}{kanerva}{1024} of these queries, and on \Inj{repomem-holdout-plain}{kanerva}{1024} on the holdout repositories, but the simple rule of keeping the newest near-twin reaches \Inj{repomem-plain}{hybrid-dedup}{1024} and \Inj{repomem-holdout-plain}{hybrid-dedup}{1024}, with recall \R{repomem-plain}{hybrid-dedup}{1024} on the test repositories. So on RepoMem the gain comes from that rule, which \kanerva also uses, and not from its probabilistic model. Removing the rule from \kanerva brings injection back to \Inj{repomem-plain}{-supersession}{1024}. We have no ablation that isolates the hazard model's value here.

The rule depends on near-identical text. RepoMem's versions differ only in a value, which favors it. On the natural-language updates of LongMemEval, the evidence for a fact and for its update are not near-twins, the rule does not fire, and \kanerva injects the outdated turn on \Inj{lme-updates}{kanerva}{1024} of questions against \Inj{lme-updates}{hybrid}{1024} for hybrid. Its error there is \Err{lme-updates}{kanerva}{1024}, against \Err{lme-updates}{hybrid}{1024}. Listing items newest first does help, taking hybrid's error to \Err{lme-updates}{hybrid-newest}{1024}, so the plugin lists injected notes that way. Detecting that a free-text statement replaces an older one remains unsolved here, and it likely needs semantics that an LLM-free method lacks.

\subsection{Ablations}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} removes one component at a time. After correction, only cost density on LongMemEval-S at 1024 tokens is significant, and the clustered datasets have too few units to reach significance. Density is decisive there and slightly harmful elsewhere: on LoCoMo removing it adds up to \V{dens-locomo} recall at small budgets. Validity has almost no effect on recall on chat benchmarks. On RepoMem it helps at small budgets, by freeing budget that stale versions would use, and costs about a point without keys at 1024 tokens; its main effect is on staleness, which recall does not show. Facets add less than a point. The facility-location term and activation have effects indistinguishable from zero. We keep them because they are cheap, and because activation only becomes informative with real access histories, which these benchmarks lack, but this benchmark set does not establish their value.

\subsection{A disclosed post-hoc change}\label{sec:posthoc}
The first test run showed \kanerva below hybrid on keyed RepoMem, and the ablation pointed at soft supersession: signatures of different functions are near-duplicates, so they discounted each other. Since keyed items have exact version chains, we restricted soft supersession to items without a key. This fix was found on test data, so numbers on those six repositories are no longer clean. Table~\ref{tab:recall} reports both the first-run rule and the fix. To check it fairly we added four repositories after that run (starlette, typer, black, aiohttp), never used for tuning. There, at 1024 tokens, the fixed method has recall \R{repomem-holdout}{kanerva}{1024} against \R{repomem-holdout}{kanerva-first}{1024} for the first-run rule, \R{repomem-holdout}{hybrid}{1024} for hybrid and \R{repomem-holdout}{hybrid-live}{1024} for hybrid with invalidation. LongMemEval and LoCoMo have no keys, so the change does not touch them.

\subsection{Checks of the theory on synthetic instances}
With known ground truth (eight seeds, horizon 4000, 40 items per round, three classes with different hazards), cumulative regret against the per-round greedy optimum is \V{regret-known} for a learner that knows validity, \V{regret-estimated} when it estimates hazards from censored lifetimes, and \V{regret-blind} when it is blind. The log--log slopes in $T$ are \V{slope-known}, \V{slope-estimated} and \V{slope-blind}. The blind learner's linear growth is what Proposition~\ref{prop:blind} predicts, and it shows the value of the separation. The observed regret of the other two is far below the upper bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:regret}, whose value here exceeds the trivial bound, so these runs do not test the bound's tightness. Over \V{certn} random selection problems, $F(S)$ was on average \V{certmean} of the certificate upper bound, with a minimum of \V{certmin}, above the worst-case constant of $0.316$.

\paragraph{Multi-resolution.} Injecting query-biased excerpts of long items, with a pointer to the full text, raises the number of items that fit. On LongMemEval-S at 512 tokens, the share of evidence turns reached moves from \V{mr-0@512} with full items to \V{mr-48@512} with excerpts of items above 48 tokens, and at 1024 tokens from \V{mr-0@1024} to \V{mr-48@1024}. This counts an excerpt as reaching the turn. Whether the excerpt contains the answer, or the agent would expand it, was not measured without an LLM.

\subsection{Latency and scale}
On an M1 Pro with an uncontended CPU, a fresh query at $10^4$ items takes \V{p50-hybrid@10000} ms at the median and \V{p95-hybrid@10000} ms at the 95th percentile, plus \V{encode} ms to embed the query, and lexical retrieval takes \V{p50-lexical@10000} and \V{p95-lexical@10000} ms. At $10^5$ items the medians are \V{p50-hybrid@100000} and \V{p50-lexical@100000} ms. A write costs \V{wr-hybrid@10000} ms, excluding the embedding. The memory layer spends no LLM tokens, so its token cost is the injected context alone.

\section{Problem}\label{sec:framework}

\paragraph{Memory.} At time $t$ the store is a finite set $M_t$. An item is a tuple $m=(\mathrm{text},e,\tau^{\mathrm{c}},\tau^{\mathrm{v}},\pi,c,k,P)$: content, unit embedding, creation time, the last time the item was confirmed, provenance, token cost $c(m)\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}$, an optional \emph{slot key} $k$ that names the fact the item states, and links $P$ to the items it was consolidated from. Items that share a key form a version chain.

\paragraph{Validity and usefulness.} For a query $q$, let $\rho_t(m)=\Pr[m \text{ still holds at } t]$ and $\mu_q(m)=\Pr[m\text{ helps with }q\mid m\text{ holds}]$. The \emph{effective weight} of an item is $w_q(m)=\rho_t(m)\,\mu_q(m)$. The two factors move for different reasons: $\rho$ changes with time and with events in the world, $\mu$ with the query. A similarity score can express neither of them on its own, and Section~\ref{sec:theory} shows what is lost when they are merged.

\paragraph{Selection.} Injecting $S\subseteq M_t$ has utility $U(S\mid q)$, and the context budget is a hard constraint:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:budgeted}
  \max_{S\subseteq M_t}\ \mathbb{E}\,[U(S\mid q_t)]\quad\text{s.t.}\quad c(S):=\sum_{m\in S}c(m)\le B .
\end{equation}
When $w$ is a probability of being needed, the expected number of needed items in $S$ is additive,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
  F(S)=\sum_{m\in S}w_q(m),
\end{equation}
and \eqref{eq:budgeted} becomes a 0--1 knapsack. This is the objective the system optimises, over candidates and with estimated weights. A richer objective could reward covering several facets of a query, or penalise near-duplicates, and would be submodular; Section~\ref{sec:theory} says what would change.

\paragraph{A memory policy.} A policy decides what to write, what to read, what to consolidate and what to forget. Reading costs tokens at every step, writing costs LLM calls or latency, and the store costs retrieval time and noise. This paper is about the read path and about how it adapts to feedback. Model weights never change.

\subsection{Where these methods lose}
Three elementary worst cases show the design defects a better read path has to remove. Write $\mathrm{OPT}$ for the optimum of \eqref{eq:budgeted}.

\begin{proposition}[Cost blindness]\label{prop:cost}
Any rule that fills the budget in the order of a score independent of $c$ (top-$k$ fill, MMR, reciprocal rank fusion, reranking) has worst-case ratio at most $1/\lfloor B/c_{\min}\rfloor$ against $\mathrm{OPT}$, even for additive $U$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Recency as proxy]\label{prop:recency}
A relevant item $m$ with $c(m)\le B$, followed by later items of total cost above $B$, each no larger than $m$, is never injected by recency-only selection, however relevant it is, whereas a rule that scores by relevance injects it whenever it outranks those items.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Time-invariant scoring]\label{prop:stale}
Let two versions of a fact have equal similarity to the query, and let their timestamps be assigned uniformly. A selector whose score does not depend on timestamps ranks the outdated version first with probability $1/2$.
\end{proposition}

Every $k$-limited row of Table~\ref{tab:special} is cost-blind, and every row with no validity model or a recency proxy falls under Proposition~\ref{prop:recency} or~\ref{prop:stale}. Zep and Graphiti avoid Proposition~\ref{prop:stale} only for facts where the extractor produced the invalidating edge. Additive selection, ours included, can also spend its budget on near-duplicates, which a coverage objective would avoid in the worst case; on our data that case made no measurable difference.
